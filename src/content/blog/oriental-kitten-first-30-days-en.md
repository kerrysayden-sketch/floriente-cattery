Kitten's home. The carrier sits in the hallway. Inside — silence.

The first 30 days aren't about bonding. They're about giving the kitten room to build a map of the new world: one room at a time, at its own pace. The 3-3-3 rule: 3 days for decompression, 3 weeks for adjustment, 3 months to feel at home. Rush it — and you'll lose trust that takes months to rebuild.

## The Short Version

Days 1–3: one small room, carrier open, your visits short — don't push contact

Days 4–7: the kitten starts short ventures out, when it decides

Week 2: feeding routine locks in, 2–3 play sessions a day

Weeks 3–4: introductions to the smells of other animals, gradual access to the whole home, the real personality starts coming out

24 hours without food is a vet emergency, not "settling in"

Important: this is one way things can unfold. Some kittens react differently — and that's fine too. More on individual reactions later in the article.

## Days 1–3: The Safe Room

Set up one small room before the kitten arrives — a bathroom works well. Put the carrier inside, open the door, and don't touch it. The kitten will come out when it's ready. Don't reach in. Don't try to lure it.

Elvira tells owners simply: "You walk in, set it down in the bathroom, open the carrier, and wait" — walked in, set down, opened, wait.

AAFP (American Association of Feline Practitioners) defines 5 basic environmental needs: food and water (in different spots), litter box, scratching post, hideouts, vertical space. All of this should be in the safe room from hour one. Don't put food and water bowls next to the litter box — it goes against basic instinct and raises stress.

The carrier stays the main hideout for the first 72 hours. Don't take it away. A cardboard box with a hole cut on the side gives the same effect.

Walk into the room quietly, sit on the floor, talk softly, walk out. Don't force eye contact — wait for the kitten to come to you. 3 days of decompression is the minimum for a confident kitten. For an anxious one — 7 to 14 days in the safe room is normal.

## Days 4–7: First Ventures Out

On day four, if the kitten is eating reliably, using the litter box, and approaching you on its own — you can open the safe room door.

Don't carry the kitten into the next room. Don't fling the door open and walk away. Sit next to the open door. The kitten will sniff the threshold, turn back, come up again. That's normal — that's how it builds confidence, not fear.

One room at a time. A kitten that finds itself in a large apartment on day five will retreat and lose ground it had already gained. Positive associations matter: drop a treat just past the safe room door. Play with a wand toy in the hallway. Let curiosity do its work.

"It's like a child" — that's how Elvira describes the first days. A child learning to walk falls and looks at the adult: what's the reaction? The kitten does the same. A calm adult nearby — a safety signal. An anxious adult looming over — a danger signal.

By day seven, the kitten should be eating reliably, using the litter box without hesitation, and spending time outside the carrier even when you're around.

## Two Reaction Types: Both Are Normal

The protocol above is one version. We've seen two very different scenarios with our owners, and both ended in healthy, happy cats. Watch your kitten closely — and don't try to force it into someone else's pace.

### Type A — the slow, cautious one

Seba lived in the bathroom for a full week, then another week on the second floor, and every next room needed its own week. The first time in any new space — "crouches" near every threshold. Cautious by nature. He wasn't less healthy or less happy — his pace is just slow.

### Type B — the explorer

Halva spent 10–15 minutes in the safe room and decided that was enough. She wanted to sniff the whole apartment at once. Keeping her in one room was impossible. She demanded you walk along and show her every room. It was easier for her once she'd seen everything.

### Another real example: kitten behind the stove

One of our kittens spent the first night behind the stove. The owners called: "It's cold there, what do we do?" I said: "Don't worry. When it gets cold, it'll come out. Don't pull it out by force." During the night it came out to eat, then hid again. By day three, it was sleeping in bed with the owners. It just needed its own pace — and it set that pace itself.

### What this means for you

The cautious one will grow into the most affectionate, gentle cat — the one who knows your every mood. The explorer will grow into a smart, bold, curious personality. It's not "this one's right, that one's wrong" — it's two different temperaments, both normal. Watch your kitten, not someone else's protocol. Your job is to create the conditions; it'll set the pace.

## Week 2: The Routine Forms

Orientals live by predictability. Week 2 is when the schedule for the next 15 years locks in.

Feed at the same time every day. The exact hour matters less than consistency — pick a time that's realistic on weekends too. Refresh the water fountain on schedule. Clean the litter box at the same time.

Start 2–3 play sessions a day — as many as your schedule allows. Minimum — one in the morning, one in the evening. Each — 10–15 minutes, wand toy or feather, the full hunting sequence: stalking, chase, capture, final grab. End each session with a small portion of food — that closes the hunting cycle and brings down the post-play arousal. Orientals are athletic and smart; minimum 2 sessions isn't a luxury, it's a behavioral necessity.

Watch the litter box closely this week. Straining, frequent trips with no result, or blood in the urine — these are same-day emergencies. A kitten circling the litter box and unable to pass urine needs a vet within hours — urinary obstruction is life-threatening.

## Weeks 3–4: Full Integration

In the third week, the 3-3-3 rule reaches its second mark: 3 weeks of stable, safe acquaintance — and a kitten that's already mapped the space starts showing its real personality. What you see now is closer to the adult cat you'll live with for the next decade.

If there are other animals in the home — don't rush. Start with scent exchange: swap bedding between animals without visual contact for 5–7 days. Then introduce through a barrier — a baby gate or a cracked door — before sharing space. Hissing at the gate is normal. A kitten that stops eating because of the other animal's presence — isn't.

Full apartment access — when the kitten runs confidently between rooms, not creeps. Before opening up new spaces, remove hazards: loose cords, toxic plants, gaps behind appliances, unsecured windows.

3 months — the third mark of the 3-3-3 rule: full behavioral settling. Until then, expect regressions during stress (vet visits, guests, thunderstorms). That's normal.

## Shopping Checklist Before Arrival

"The scratching post is priority number one" — no debate. A scratching post that's there on arrival day gets used from day one. One ordered later competes with the couch.

Before the kitten shows up at home:

☐ Scratching post (height for a full stretch — minimum 60 cm, sisal preferred)

☐ Litter box + litter — check with the breeder which one they used. Get the same size and the same litter at first (later, as the kitten gets used to it, you can gradually switch to one that's more convenient for you). Minimum — 1 box per cat plus one spare; unscented, clumping.

☐ Bowls for food and water — in different spots, not next to each other

☐ Carrier — leave it open with familiar bedding, don't put it away

☐ Minimum 2 hideouts (box, cat house, tunnel with a roof)

☐ Wand toy and small toys for solo play

☐ Blanket or item with the smell of the cattery — place near the sleeping spot

☐ Enzyme cleaner for cleaning up "accidents"

Helpful but not essential for week one: a pheromone diffuser installed in the safe room before the kitten arrives.

## When to Call the Vet

Not every behavior in the first 30 days is cause for alarm. Hiding is normal, as long as the kitten is eating. Hissing at new stimuli is normal. One soft stool after travel is normal. The following — isn't:

| Behavior | Status | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Hiding, but eating and using the litter box | Normal stress reaction | Observe; do nothing |
| 24 hours without food | Vet emergency | Take to the vet the same day |
| Frequent trips to the litter box without passing urine | Life-threatening | Vet immediately, within hours |
| Repeated vomiting (3+ times a day) | Medical issue | Vet the same day |
| Open-mouth breathing at rest | Emergency | Vet immediately |
| Discharge from eyes or nose | Medical issue | Vet within 24 hours |
| Loose stool more than 2 days | Needs evaluation | Take to the vet for a checkup |

For kittens under 6 weeks (doesn't apply to Floriente kittens, who leave at the right age, but matters for those taking in strays): the critical no-food window shrinks to 12–18 hours. Hypoglycemia in very young kittens develops quickly and severely.

## Direct Talk

A kitten that doesn't eat for more than 24 hours is a vet emergency. Not "settling in."

Orientals are high-metabolism cats. They can't go without food for long without physical consequences. Hepatic lipidosis (fatty liver disease) can begin as early as 48 hours without food. If a kitten hasn't eaten for 24 hours — call the vet. Don't wait "maybe tomorrow it'll be better."

This applies even when the kitten is drinking water. Drinking without eating isn't enough. The threshold is 24 hours. Not 36, not 48.

## Frequent Questions

**The kitten hid under the bed and won't come out. Is that normal?**

For days 1–7 — it's a normal decompression reaction. Normal hiding looks like this: at night the kitten comes out, eats, uses the litter box, returns. Abnormal — the kitten doesn't eat for 24 hours, doesn't use the litter box, or has trouble breathing. If food is disappearing and the litter box is being used — let it set the pace.

**When should I introduce the kitten to a dog?**

No earlier than week two, and only after 5–7 days of scent exchange. The order matters: the kitten needs full access to the safe room and at least partial access to the apartment before the dog enters the kitten's space — not the other way around. The kitten has to know its escape routes. The full introduction protocol is in [Oriental Cats with Kids, Dogs, and Other Cats](/en/blog/oriental-cat-with-dogs-and-kids/).

**The kitten cried all night. What do I do?**

Vocalizing at night is normal for the first 3–5 days. Orientals are a communicative breed, naturally loud. A clock wrapped in a blanket near the sleeping spot can help.

**How do I tell if the kitten is eating enough?**

Weigh the kitten every 2–3 days during the first month on a kitchen scale. If weight is stable or going up — it's eating enough. If weight loss exceeds 10% in a week — call the vet.

**Should I bathe the kitten in the first week?**

No. Unless the kitten arrived visibly dirty, a bath in the first week adds unnecessary stress. The first bath, if needed, comes no earlier than week two, once the kitten has built basic trust in you and the space.

**The kitten uses the litter box but also scratches outside it. What's wrong?**

Possibly the box. Orientals demand cleanliness — clean it at least once a day. If the box is clean and the behavior continues — check the size (the kitten should be able to turn around freely) and the location (not next to food, not in a noisy spot). Covered boxes trap odor and can put cats off.

**When will I see the kitten's real personality?**

In the third week, the stress layer starts to peel away. By the third month, you're meeting the cat you'll live with for years. Some Orientals keep their distance for the first 30 days, and then become cats that follow you everywhere. Give the process time.

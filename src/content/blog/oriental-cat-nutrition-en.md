The kitten's home. Now the most important daily decision you'll make for the next 15+ years starts today: what's in the bowl.

Orientals and Siamese burn energy faster than most breeds. An adult cat needs 250–400 kcal a day — mostly from animal protein, not grain or plant fillers. Get it right, and you'll have a lean, loud, opinionated cat. Get it wrong, and the consequences arrive quietly — then suddenly.

## The short answer

Orientals are obligate carnivores: 35–45% of calories should come from animal protein.

Wet food — 70% of the daily ration, dry — 30%. This isn't a taste preference, it's a hydration strategy.

Rotation — minimum 5 brands. This builds GI resilience and prevents food fixation.

Never: onion, garlic, grapes, lilies, chocolate, xylitol — some of these cause irreversible organ failure within hours.

Safe nutrition has three equally important components: safety, balance, and feeding psychology.

Raw isn't forbidden — but balancing it at home without a nutritionist is hard. Commercial food handles that for you.

## Why nutrition is so full of contradictions

Ask ten cat owners — you'll get twelve answers. One swears by dry food. Another considers it poison. Someone's grinding mice from a forum recipe. Someone else feeds the cat what they eat themselves. Each one is convinced their version is the right one.

The problem isn't the people — it's that the question is framed wrong. "What to feed?" isn't one answer. It's three criteria that all have to line up at once. If you can see those three criteria, you can evaluate any diet — natural, commercial, mixed, BARF — and tell whether it works for your specific cat.

Below are those three criteria, in the order given by the veterinary nutritionist whose materials we built this article on.

## The three pillars of proper nutrition

Any diet — from the most expensive food to homemade meat with vegetables — has to pass three checks. This isn't a sequence, it's three axes at once. Failing one is failing the whole diet.

### 1. Safety

This is the baseline — what you're actually giving the cat. Not poisoned, not spoiled, not contaminated.

For commercial food, safety means: a fresh batch, a normal expiration date, proper storage after opening (sealed container, not in the sun), and a popular brand (because it moves through warehouses fast and doesn't sit there "aging" for years).

For raw and BARF, safety is harder — it starts with the meat source. In Ukrainian conditions we don't have an EU-style quality system: it's hard to guarantee meat doesn't contain antibiotic residue or salmonella. Freezing doesn't fully solve it — some bacteria can only be killed by heat treatment.

Safety also means raw eggs can't be given long-term (enzymes destroy vitamin B), raw river fish — never (thiaminase and parasites), raw pork — carefully. This isn't dogma, it's biochemistry.

### 2. Balance

A balanced diet isn't "meat plus a little vegetables." A nutritionist tracks over 50 metrics: protein, fat, carbs, fiber, 12+ vitamins, 12+ minerals, amino acids (taurine, arginine, methionine, cysteine and others), omega-3 and omega-6 fatty acids in the right ratio.

Complete commercial food (with AAFCO or FEDIAF certification on the package) has already balanced all these metrics — that's its core value. Behind every brand stand departments of felinologists, veterinary nutritionists, technologists. They calculate the proportions so the cat eats everything together, not just the tasty bits.

A homemade diet built from an internet recipe rarely passes this check. Most often it's short on calcium, iodine, zinc, omega-3. Sometimes it overdoses vitamin A or E (and vitamin A is dangerous in excess). In BARF diets with bones, you add the risk of intestinal obstruction — a documented surgical problem, not a theoretical one.

A balanced homemade diet is possible — but it requires an individual calculation by a veterinary nutritionist, not a universal recipe. And recalculation when the cat's weight, activity, or age changes.

### 3. Feeding psychology

This is the least-discussed component — and it's where most diets that looked right on paper fall apart.

Feeding psychology means routine, predictability, and respect for what the cat will actually eat. You can prepare a perfectly balanced dish, and the cat eats only the meat and leaves the vegetables and supplements in the bowl. Now the diet isn't balanced anymore — no matter how it was designed.

Orientals are especially sensitive to routine. 24/7 free-feeding often leads to problems with them — they gain weight faster than most breeds, or, conversely, ignore food and lose weight from stress. Scheduled feeding — 2–3 times a day at fixed times — stabilizes both appetite and behavior. Exception: if the cat eats poorly or is recovering from stress or a move, you can temporarily leave a small amount of dry food available, gradually returning to scheduled feeding.

The second part of psychology is respect for individuality. One cat loves dry texture, another — pâté, a third only picks chunks in jelly. Fighting it is pointless. Find the format the cat actually eats, within the first two criteria (safe and balanced).

And the third — don't feed from the table. Not as a prohibition, but as a rule of predictability: a cat who gets a piece during every dinner of yours starts demanding food constantly and loses basic appetite regulation. Treats — separately, in fixed amounts, not from the table.

## Oriental metabolism — why it matters

Orientals and Siamese aren't just small cats. They carry a high-efficiency metabolism in a lean body with minimal fat reserves to compensate for nutritional gaps.

An adult Oriental needs 250–400 kcal a day depending on weight and activity level. Those calories should come mostly from animal protein (meat, poultry, fish — not plant substitutes): 35–45% of total calories. Cats don't have the enzymatic pathways to convert plant protein into the amino acids they need. This is basic obligate carnivore biology, confirmed by WSAVA, AAFCO, and FEDIAF.

Two nutrients carry elevated risk if they're missing:

Taurine — an amino acid found only in meat and fish (it doesn't exist in plants). Required for normal heart function. Foods meeting AAFCO/FEDIAF standards already contain the right taurine dose — one of the reasons commercial food beats an unsupervised homemade diet.

Arginine — another amino acid the cat gets from meat and fish. It's involved in liver function. AAFCO/FEDIAF-standard foods contain it in the right proportions.

That's why the food you choose matters at the level of composition, not just the brand.

## Wet vs. dry — the right balance

WSAVA's recommendations are unambiguous: cats evolved as desert animals with a low thirst threshold. Most of their hydration comes from prey, not a water bowl. Wet food — 70–85% moisture — recreates that biological reality. Dry food — 8–12% moisture — doesn't.

The practical consequence: cats fed exclusively dry food live in a state of chronic mild dehydration. Over years, this raises the risk of lower urinary tract disease (FLUTD) and chronic kidney disease — two leading causes of premature death in housecats.

The right ratio: 70% wet food, 30% dry by calories.

Dry food isn't useless. It has a role in puzzle toys that slow down eating and provide mental stimulation. Some cats also prefer its texture for part of a meal. But it's a supplement to wet food, not a replacement.

Practical tip: read the guaranteed analysis on every can or bag. AAFCO and FEDIAF "complete and balanced" labeling indicates the food meets minimum standards for the appropriate life stage. "Supplemental" or "treat" — doesn't, and you can't build daily feeding around it.

## Rotational feeding — how and why

Feeding the same food for years creates fixation. A cat who eats only one brand or one protein is a cat you won't be able to switch when the manufacturer changes the formula, the product disappears, or a reaction develops.

At Floriente, rotation runs across 6 brands: Purina Pro Plan, Royal Canin, N&D, Brit, Optimeal, Savory. This isn't guesswork. It's a deliberate strategy for building GI resilience and preventing food dependency.

Rotation works through a 7-day transition protocol when introducing any new food:

| Days | Old food | New food |
|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | 75% | 25% |
| 3–4 | 50% | 50% |
| 5–6 | 25% | 75% |
| 7+ | — | 100% |

A faster transition risks digestive upset: loose stool, vomiting, refusing food. Slower is fine. Some cats with sensitive digestion need 10–14 days.

One reserved note about natural feeding: "I experimented with raw — it didn't work." The homemade raw experiment at Floriente didn't pan out. The reason: balancing a natural diet the way commercial foods do is practically impossible at home. Manufacturers have entire departments — felinologists, nutritionists, technologists — calculating ratios of meat, amino acids, vitamins, and adding it all so the cat eats it together. When you cook at home, the cat picks only what tastes good: a piece of meat — yes, all the important add-ins — no. Guessing how much of what's needed actually got eaten, and hitting daily balance, is a full-time job for a nutritionist, not a side task for an owner.

## Raw, commercial, or mixed — how to choose

Now that you have the three-criteria framework, pick the tool that closes it.

### Commercial food — the optimum for most

If you don't plan to make feeding the cat a separate project, complete commercial food (wet + dry, AAFCO or FEDIAF certified) is the safest option. It handles balance automatically, safety depends only on good storage, and you set up psychology with routine. This isn't a compromise or a "worse" option. It's a thought-out system that frees you from daily calculations. Many of our graduates live 12+ years exclusively on commercial food — with no health problems.

### Raw — possible, but takes work

A natural diet isn't forbidden — and veterinary nutritionists allow it under certain conditions. The catch is just that those conditions are more serious than they look on forums.

What you need for safe raw: access to quality meat (a reliable supplier, batch documentation if possible — not trivial in Ukrainian conditions); one full diet calculation by a veterinary nutritionist — not an "internet recipe," but an individual calculation for your cat's weight, age, and activity; recalculation when the cat's weight or age changes (kitten → adult, adult → senior), or health status; stable fat content — sudden swings (chicken neck today, breast tomorrow) hit the cat's GI tract; separate vitamin supplements as prescribed by a vet (calcium, omega-3, sometimes others).

If you're ready for that — raw can work. If not — it's not "almost raw," it's an unbalanced diet that will eventually surface as health problems.

### BARF — its own story

BARF (Biologically Appropriate Raw Food) — raw diets with meat, organs, bones, sometimes vegetables. The concept: imitating what the cat would eat in the wild.

What you should know: bones in BARF are the main surgical risk. Even experienced vets have dealt with intestinal obstruction from bones that "should have digested fine." Better without bones, or only soft ground bones, never whole. Raw meat in Ukrainian conditions — same salmonella, antibiotic, and possible parasite risk. Off-the-shelf "BARF recipes from the internet" rarely pass a nutritionist's check — they're short on calcium, iodine, omega-3, sometimes overdosed on vitamin A.

Some vets are okay with BARF if a nutritionist (not a forum) does the calculation and the meat source is tightly controlled.

### Mixed diet — food + meat

The most common compromise: commercial food as the base, plus home-cooked meat as a supplement. There's a clear rule here that often gets skipped: meat as a supplement — no more than 10% of daily calories. Past that, you break the balance of the commercial food and have to recalculate the whole diet as natural.

That means: for an adult Oriental at a daily 250–400 kcal — extra meat in the 25–40 kcal range. That's roughly 30–50 g of cooked chicken or turkey breast (no skin, no bones). If you want to give more — you've effectively switched to a mixed diet that needs to be balanced as a whole, like raw.

## Feeding by age

Nutritional needs change throughout a cat's life. Below — practical benchmarks for Orientals and Siamese.

## Specific foods: what's allowed, what's not, what's a myth

Most of the "rules" about cat food circulating on forums and at the market don't hold up. Here are the main points.

### Chicken — an allergen?

Myth. Chicken is the most accessible, easily digestible protein. Yes, individual chicken allergies happen — same as with any other protein. But chicken isn't "an allergen by definition." If your cat reacts normally to it — it's a complete protein source.

### Pork — forbidden?

Not forbidden, but as the base of the diet — no. Fatty cuts of pork (neck, bacon) are too heavy for cats. Lean cuts (tenderloin) in small amounts are fine. As a backup option in case of chicken or beef allergy, pork stays available.

### Potato — forbidden?

Boiled potato without skin — fine as a small supplement. Raw green potato with sprouts — definitely not (contains solanine — toxic). Potato shouldn't be the base of the diet, but as a small additional element — no problem.

### Corn — poison?

Myth. Corn in commercial foods is processed for digestion and doesn't cause harm. Boiled corn, which many cats love, is fine as a treat. "Corn isn't digested" is a surface observation: you could say the same about carrots or zucchini, but they still do their fiber job.

### Raw egg for coat?

Definitely not — especially long-term. Raw egg white contains avidin, which destroys vitamin B (biotin). Regular raw egg leads to biotin deficiency over time. Cooked egg — fine as a supplement, not the base.

### Milk — definitely no?

Not exactly. Adult milk (whole cow) in small amounts is tolerated normally by many cats. But fermented dairy (kefir, plain yogurt, cottage cheese) is better: easier to digest and helps with dry stool or constipation. Sour cream — too fatty for a regular addition. One important principle: the fat percentage of dairy should stay stable. If you give 1% kefir, give 1% consistently — don't switch to 5%. Fat-content swings hit the GI tract.

### Seafood and fish

Salmon and herring — a good source of omega-3, can be given cooked (not raw) 1–2 times a week. Shrimp, squid, mussels — small amounts, cooked, as treats — fine if there's no allergy. Raw river fish — forbidden (thiaminase destroys vitamin B1, parasites). Fatty fish in large amounts also isn't worth it — it disrupts mineral balance through excess phosphorus.

### Vegetables — how to give

A cat is an obligate carnivore — vegetables aren't an energy source. But fiber is needed for gut function. Pumpkin (boiled), zucchini, carrot, boiled broccoli — small amounts are fine. If the cat refuses raw vegetables — boil and chop them, mix with meat.

### Treats

Store-bought "colorful" treats — read the ingredients. Glycerin, sorbitol, artificial dyes — not poison, but not useful either. Better to go natural: a piece of cooked meat, dried udder or lung (in small amounts because of diarrhea risk from connective tissue). General rule: 2–3 treat varieties per cat is enough. No more than 10% of daily calories as treats — past that, the balance of the main diet breaks.

## Forbidden foods — no compromises

This isn't a list of foods cats "better not have." This is a list of substances that cause documented, irreversible harm.

| Food / Substance | Risk level | Mechanism and effect |
|---|---|---|
| Onion and garlic | HIGH | Oxidative damage to red blood cells → hemolytic anemia. All forms: raw, cooked, powder. |
| Lilies (all parts) | LETHAL | Acute kidney failure. Onset: 24–72 hours after contact. Even water from the vase. |
| Chocolate | HIGH | Theobromine → cardiac arrhythmia, seizures, death. Dark chocolate = highest concentration. |
| Grapes and raisins | HIGH | Mechanism unknown; outcome documented: acute kidney failure. No safe dose established. |
| Xylitol (sweetener) | HIGH | Severe hypoglycemia → liver failure. Found in sugar-free gum, some peanut butters, baked goods. |
| Alcohol (any) | HIGH | Rapid CNS depression → coma. Toxic dose is extremely small relative to body weight. |
| Raw fish (regularly) | MEDIUM | Contains thiaminase — an enzyme that destroys vitamin B1 (thiamine). Neurological damage with chronic exposure. |
| Cow's milk | LOW–MEDIUM | Most adult cats are lactose-intolerant. GI upset, diarrhea. Not acutely toxic, but unnecessary. |

If you suspect ingestion of any HIGH or LETHAL category item: don't wait for symptoms. Contact a vet or animal poison control immediately.

## Water — the hidden problem

Cats with access only to still water in a regular bowl drink less than cats with running water. The difference isn't small: fountains increase voluntary water intake by 50–70% compared to a still bowl. This is an established behavioral fact, not a fountain marketing claim.

Daily water requirement for an adult Oriental is 40–60 ml per kilogram of body weight. A 4 kg cat needs 160–240 ml a day. A cat eating 70% wet food gets a significant chunk from food. A cat on mostly dry food — doesn't.

Practical setup that works:

Ceramic or metal fountain — plastic bowls accumulate biofilm and can cause chin acne in cats.

Wide, shallow bowl — cats prefer their whiskers not to touch the sides.

Separate from the food bowl — in nature, cats don't drink where they eat (a contamination-avoidance instinct).

Change water daily — clean the fountain filter once a week.

If your cat's urine is dark yellow or strong-smelling — water intake is too low. That's a clinical signal, not an aesthetic preference.

## Vitamins and supplements: when needed, when not

If the cat's on a complete commercial food — extra vitamins aren't needed without a vet's prescription. Manufacturers add vitamins with a small margin since they partially break down in storage. If you pile your own on top — you'll get an overdose, not a benefit.

If the cat's on a natural diet — supplements are calculated individually. Most often needed: calcium (regular calcium citrate from the pharmacy, dose set by a nutritionist by weight); omega-3 (salmon oil or human fish oil capsules — veterinary blends are sometimes overloaded with vitamins A and D); iodine and zinc, if the diet is clearly short on them. Cat multivitamin complexes — use carefully — sometimes calcium in them is too low even at the recommended dose.

Vitamins A and D are the most dangerous in overdose. This isn't "more is better." Vitamin A overdose hits bones and liver, vitamin D — kidneys. That's why DIY combinations of human vitamins for a cat are extra risk.

If you give fermented dairy (kefir, cottage cheese) — it's not a supplement in the technical sense, but it's useful for gut function, especially with constipation tendencies. Stable amount, stable fat content.

## If the cat gains weight: what to do

Orientals are lean by nature, but the wrong diet or stress can lead to weight gain. Safe weight loss is slow and controlled.

Algorithm: (1) calculate how much food the cat is currently eating per day — that's the baseline; (2) reduce the portion by 10% — not 50%, because a sudden drop is shock starvation that doesn't produce weight loss and sometimes triggers hepatic lipidosis in lean breeds; (3) hold for 2 weeks, observe, weigh under the same conditions (morning before feeding); (4) safe rate of loss is 0.5–1% of body mass per week: for a 5 kg cat that's 25–50 g, only visible on a kitchen scale; (5) if there's no movement after 2–3 weeks — drop another 10%; (6) if you reach the line where the cat would be undersupplied with protein — see a veterinary nutritionist.

What not to do: starve the cat (Orientals with their high metabolism can't go 24–48 hours without food — hepatic lipidosis risk); slash the portion in half (this doesn't speed up weight loss, just causes stress); replace the main diet with "diet" treats — that's not a diet, that's marketing.

## FAQ

**Can I feed an Oriental only dry food — it's more convenient?**

No. Dry food at 8–12% moisture keeps cats in chronic dehydration. Over months and years this raises the risk of kidney disease and FLUTD. The 70/30 wet/dry ratio is a minimum standard, not a preference.

**How do I know a food is "complete and balanced"?**

Look for AAFCO or FEDIAF compliance labeling on the package. It'll specify the life stage the food is designed for. "Supplemental" food doesn't have this labeling — you can't feed it as the main diet.

**The cat refuses the new food. What do I do?**

Go back to the previous ratio in the 7-day protocol. Some cats need 10–14 days, not 7. Don't skip meals to "make them hungry enough" — cats who don't eat for 24–48 hours risk hepatic lipidosis. Especially lean breeds like Orientals.

**Is grain-free food better for Orientals?**

Not automatically. "Grain-free" is a marketing claim that doesn't guarantee a better diet. What matters more is the quality of the protein source (meat, fish — not plant substitutes) and overall balance to AAFCO/FEDIAF standard. Read the guaranteed analysis on the package, not the marketing promises on the label.

**Can I feed the cat from the table?**

No, not as a main diet. People and cats have different digestive systems: what's good for us can be hard or toxic for a cat. Plus human food is often salted, seasoned, contains onion or garlic (toxic to cats). Main feeding — certified food (AAFCO/FEDIAF) that covers all needs.

**Can I give raw meat as a treat?**

Occasionally — a small amount of plain cooked chicken or turkey carries low risk. Raw meat carries pathogen risk (Salmonella, Toxoplasma), and regular raw fish — thiaminase exposure. "Treat" isn't the same as "safe daily supplement."

**How do I know the cat is eating enough?**

Weigh the cat monthly. An adult Oriental should hold a stable weight with 5–10% variation. Unmotivated weight loss over 4–6 weeks is a reason for a vet check regardless of appetite.

**When do I switch from kitten to adult food?**

For Orientals — at 12 months. Kitten foods are higher-calorie and richer in protein for growth. Continuing them past 12 months means a calorie surplus in a cat whose growth has already stopped.

**How many times a day to feed an adult cat?**

An adult Oriental — 2–3 times a day at fixed times. A kitten under 6 months — 4–5 times. 24/7 free-feeding often leads to problems with Orientals: either overeating, or, conversely, ignoring food. A schedule stabilizes appetite.

**Can I give dry and wet food at the same time — in one bowl?**

In one bowl — not ideal: different textures together often go uneaten, and wet food spoils faster. Better separately: wet in the morning, dry in the evening (or the other way around), at a 70/30 ratio by calories favoring wet.

**The cat barely drinks water — what do I do?**

Check several things. First — the bowl: plastic accumulates odor, ceramic or metal works better. Second — location: separate from the food bowl, not in a corner, not near the litter box. Third — water motion: a fountain increases voluntary intake in many cats. Fourth — diet: if it's mostly dry food, the cat will drink little, because it biologically expects moisture from food. Switching to 70% wet food solves most hydration problems.

**How many grams of food per day?**

Every food package has a chart by cat weight — that's the basic reference. But real need varies ±20% from that figure depending on activity, age, sterilization. Benchmarks by weight for an adult Oriental on complete food: 3–4 kg → 40–60 g dry OR 200–280 g wet per day; 4–5 kg → 50–70 g dry OR 250–350 g wet; 5–6 kg → 60–80 g dry OR 300–400 g wet. If you mix dry and wet — reduce both proportionally, calculating by calories.

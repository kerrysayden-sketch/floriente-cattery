Oriental cats need much less than it looks. Brushing — practically not needed, claws — once a month, baths — twice a year at most. But there's one area owners skip the most often — and it's the one that eventually hits internal organs. This article closes all four zones, so nothing catches you off guard.

## The short version

Coat: a shorthaired Oriental doesn't need brushing; wiping with a damp cloth once a month is enough.

Claws: trim once a month; if the cat actively uses a scratching post — front claws wear down on their own, focus on the back paws.

Ears: weekly check; clean only when there's visible buildup.

Teeth: if the cat lets you — brush once a week; if not (most cats) — visual check + VOHC supplements + treats + dry food as an alternative, brushing with paste and professional cleaning as needed.

Bathing: 1–2 times a year, only when actually dirty, cat-specific shampoo only.

## Coat — the easiest part

The Oriental Shorthair has one of the simplest coats among all domestic cat breeds. Short, single-layer, no undercoat, no mats, no heavy shedding. That means one radical thing: daily or weekly brushing isn't needed at all.

What actually works: wiping with a damp cloth once a month. It removes dust, distributes skin oil, tones the coat. For many of our cats this is enough forever — we haven't brushed Sima at all, in all these years. Seba sometimes (his coat is a bit longer) — we run a brush over him once a month, no system to it.

Shedding in a shorthaired Oriental is practically nonexistent. Occasionally there's a small hair on the furniture — it's not the "twice-a-year heavy brushing" you do with fluffy breeds. Regular wiping with a damp cloth covers it.

Exception: the Oriental Longhair — different logic, a brush once a week is genuinely needed. But the shorthaired group at Floriente — the main one (Oriental Shorthair and Siamese) — doesn't require brushing.

On shampoo quality — a real practical lesson from our experience. Once before a show we washed a cat with regular store-bought cat shampoo — seemed harmless. At the show the coat turned out coarse, the judges noticed, points were docked. Since then we use a professional shampoo with conditioner, recommended by show groomers. The difference is significant — silky coat, glossy, natural tone. If you're planning shows or just want quality — better to consult professional groomers, it costs more but the result is fundamentally different.

Benchmark: damp cloth once a month, brush only as needed, bath once a year (sometimes less).

## Teeth — how to care realistically

Cats' teeth genuinely matter: plaque and tartar buildup over time can lead to periodontal disease, and according to WSAVA data, 70% of cats have it by age 2. That's true. What's not true — that the only way is daily brushing.

Reality: most cats don't tolerate teeth brushing. Even we, as breeders, can't get our own cats to tolerate this procedure daily. And that's fine — you don't need to wage war with the cat.

What works in real life — depending on whether the cat lets you brush:

If the cat tolerates brushing (rare): the classic procedure — a soft toothbrush or finger brush with cat toothpaste. Human toothpaste is forbidden: fluoride and xylitol are toxic to cats. Hold the brush at roughly a 45° angle to the gum line, small circular motions. Focus on the outer surfaces.

If the cat doesn't tolerate it (most cases): use alternatives that work without direct contact with the teeth.

Water additives with the VOHC seal — reduce plaque without direct contact. Dental treats — mechanical cleaning during chewing. Dry food — kibble texture has a mild abrasive effect, helps self-cleaning. Visual check once a week — look at the gums (no red inflammation) and teeth (no visible plaque or tartar).

What you definitely need: if you see yellow-brown tartar or red swollen gums — to the vet for a professional cleaning. This is done once every few years, not daily.

## Claws — shorter sessions, better results

Trim frequency — about once a month, per standard guidelines. If the cat actively uses a scratching post — natural wear on the front claws does the work, and dedicated trimming is needed less often.

Important detail: focus on the back paws. The front claws wear down on the scratching post daily; the back ones stay long and may need attention more often. Check them every few weeks — trim only if you see the claw curving inward or catching.

The scratching post's role isn't just to save the couch. It's full-on care: the cat sharpens its own claws, stretches muscles, releases stress. The right scratching post should be tall enough for the cat to fully stretch out. Vertical, stable, sisal — optimal. After that, claws stop being a weekly problem and become a regular check-in without active work.

Trimming tool: any sharp cat claw clipper. A dull blade crushes instead of cutting — that's painful and builds lasting negative association. Main rule: never cut into the pink zone — that's where the live blood vessel runs. In light-colored claws it's clearly visible; in dark claws, cut conservatively, only the curved tip.

## Ears — check weekly, clean rarely

Healthy ears barely need intervention — that's exactly why a weekly check is so valuable: it catches problems early.

What to look for: the inner surface should be pale pink with a small amount of light wax. That's normal. Reasons to pay attention: an unpleasant or unusual smell, visible redness, swelling, excessive discharge, or dark grainy buildup that looks like coffee grounds. The last one is a hallmark of ear mites.

When cleaning is needed: apply a small amount of veterinary ear cleaner to a cotton pad and gently wipe the visible part of the ear. Don't go deeper than you can see. Cotton swabs inside the ear canal — forbidden: they pack debris in and can damage the eardrum.

Ear mites, dark discharge, strong smell — that's vet territory, not home treatment. Early help is simple to resolve; advanced cases mean a deeper infection and a long course.

## Care schedule

| Area | Frequency | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Coat (brushing) | Once a month | Brush as needed |
| Ears (check) | Once a week | Clean only with visible buildup |
| Teeth | If tolerated — once a week; if not — alternatives | VOHC supplements, treats, dry food, visual check |
| Claws | Once a month | Focus on back paws (front wear down on the scratching post) |
| Bathing | Once a year | Professional cat shampoo; dry thoroughly |

## What to remember about periodontal disease

Per WSAVA, by age 2, 70% of cats have periodontal disease. For Oriental cats, with their narrow, crowded jaw, it's a bit more frequent. Infection from the oral cavity in advanced stages can enter the bloodstream and affect the heart, liver, and kidneys. So — don't torture yourself and the cat with daily brushing that doesn't work. Manage it with alternative methods (supplements, treats, dry food, visual checks) and once every few years do a professional cleaning at the vet.

## FAQ

**How often should I bathe an Oriental cat?**

The norm for a healthy indoor Oriental is once a year, sometimes less. Cats groom themselves, and excessive bathing strips the skin's natural protective layer. Bathing makes sense only when the coat is genuinely dirty — after contact with something sticky, greasy, or very dusty. Shampoo — only professional cat shampoo (human and cheap store-bought ones leave the coat coarse), and after the bath — dry thoroughly: without an undercoat the cat gets cold fast.

**The cat won't let me trim its claws. What do I do?**

Trim 1–2 claws at a time and stop before the cat starts to resist. Sessions of 2–5 minutes that end calmly build more trust than a full trim with a fight. After every session — something pleasant: a treat, play, a few minutes of calm contact. Consistency over weeks matters more than the completeness of any single session.

**How do I know if my cat has ear mites?**

Dark grainy buildup in the ear that looks like coffee grounds — the clearest sign. The cat often scratches its ears and shakes its head. Another indicator — a sharp or unusual smell. If you see any of this, go to the vet: mites spread fast and home cleaning won't fix them.

**Is dental disease really that serious for cats?**

Yes. WSAVA data: 70% of cats have periodontal disease by age 2. The bacteria involved don't stay in the mouth — as the infection progresses, they enter the bloodstream and can damage heart, liver, and kidneys. For Orientals, the narrow jaw increases tooth crowding, which speeds up plaque buildup. Dental care is the single highest-impact preventive choice for this breed.

**Can I use human toothpaste for a cat?**

No. Human paste contains fluoride and sweeteners (including xylitol), which are toxic to cats. Use only cat toothpaste — it's designed to be swallowed and comes in flavors cats accept: poultry and malt. If you haven't been able to teach brushing yet, dental products with the VOHC seal — water additives or treats — help reduce plaque until brushing becomes routine.
